Abstract

I work in computational philosophy, formal social epistemology, and the philosophy of religion. My research asks what deliberately simplified formal and computational models can teach us about epistemic and moral life in communities, and I am especially interested in cases where individual reasoning produces surprising collective outcomes.

My dissertation argues that agent-based models are powerful philosophical instruments — they reveal non-obvious mechanisms, expose hidden assumptions, test robustness, and establish coherent possibilities — but that they cannot by themselves settle questions of policy, metaphysics, or theology.

My writing sample shows that recent skeptical arguments about using track records to identify experts depend on a narrow combination of assumptions: that records are over-trusted, immediately available, and based on private evidence. Weaken any one of them and track records regain their value.

Dissertation

Flourishing in Diversity: The Promise and Limits of Agent-Based Models. My dissertation asks what philosophers can learn from agent-based models: computer simulations that show how simple interactions can produce surprising group-level outcomes. I argue that these models can clarify mechanisms, test assumptions, and establish philosophical possibilities, but only when their conclusions are matched to what the models actually support. Through case studies on expertise, the social organization of inquiry, and the soul-making response to natural evil, I show both the promise and the limits of using models in philosophical argument.

Committee: Jonathan Weisberg (chair), Franz Huber, David James Barnett, Sara Aronowitz. Supported by a SSHRC National Doctoral Award. Full abstract (PDF).

Teaching

I teach with three guiding values: clarity, in explaining ideas plainly and presenting them in more than one format; kindness, in making a classroom where students are willing to ask questions; and commitment, in being available to students and in revising a course rather than recycling it. I have been the course instructor for three courses at the University of Toronto and a teaching assistant for nineteen offerings of ten others, and I hold six teaching certificates from the university's Teaching Assistants' Training Program.

I am prepared to teach computational philosophy, formal and social epistemology, ethics of AI and technology, and philosophy of religion at the upper-year level, and introductory philosophy, critical thinking, logic, epistemology, philosophy of science, metaphysics, philosophy of mind, ethics, and bioethics at the lower-year level. Full details, evaluations, and sample materials are in my teaching dossier.
