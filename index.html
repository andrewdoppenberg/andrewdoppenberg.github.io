Abstract

I work in formal and social epistemology and the philosophy of religion, with a future second line of work in the philosophy of artificial intelligence. I use Bayesian and agent-based models (computer simulations of communities of interacting individuals) to try to answer philosophical questions.

My dissertation argues that agent-based models can reveal surprising mechanisms, expose hidden assumptions, test robustness, and establish coherent possibilities.

Dissertation

Flourishing in Diversity: The Promise and Limits of Agent-Based Models. My dissertation examines what philosophers can learn from agent-based models: Computer simulations that show how simple interactions can produce surprising group-level outcomes. I argue that these models can clarify mechanisms, test assumptions, and establish philosophical possibilities, but only when their conclusions are matched to what the models actually support. Through three case studies, two in social epistemology and one in the philosophy of religion, I show both the promise and the limits of using models in philosophical argument.

Committee: Jonathan Weisberg (supervisor), Franz Huber, David James Barnett, and Sara Aronowitz. Examiners: Justin Bruner (external) and Yonathan Fiat (internal). Supported by a SSHRC Doctoral Fellowship. The full abstract is available on request.

Teaching

I teach with three guiding values: Clarity, in explaining ideas plainly and presenting them in more than one format; kindness, in making a classroom where students are willing to ask questions; and relationality, in being available to students and in revising a course rather than repeating it unchanged. I have been the course instructor for five offerings of four courses at the University of Toronto and a teaching assistant for twenty offerings of twelve courses across the philosophy department and the Institute for the History and Philosophy of Science and Technology. I hold six teaching certificates from the university's Teaching Assistants' Training Program.

I am prepared to teach death and dying, formal epistemology, computational philosophy, social epistemology, philosophy of religion, and the ethics of AI and technology at the upper-year level, and puzzles and paradoxes, philosophy at the movies, critical reasoning, logic, introductory philosophy, epistemology, philosophy of science, philosophy of mind, metaphysics, ethics, bioethics, and the history of philosophy from ancient through analytic at the lower-year level. Full details, evaluations, and sample materials are available in my teaching dossier on request.
